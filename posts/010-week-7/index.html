What clicked

Common doesn’t mean original. I assumed the most common version of a trait must be the ancestral one: brown eyes, for example. It turns out frequency proves nothing, because selection, founder effects, and drift can all make a derived trait common. The ancestral state is found by comparing with an outgroup and reconstructing states along the tree. One part of my intuition survived: breaking is easier than building, so “no pigment” is usually the derived state (blue eyes, white eggs in some chicken breeds).

So which egg came first? The earliest dinosaur eggs were soft and unpigmented. Egg color evolved once, in eumaniraptoran dinosaurs (biliverdin for blue-green, protoporphyrin IX for red-brown), and birds inherited it. The first chicken egg was most likely beige, like the eggs of the red junglefowl.

The A in DNA and the A in ATP are almost the same thing. Both are adenine. ATP is adenine + ribose + three phosphates, and the A in RNA is the same molecule with one phosphate left: RNA polymerase builds RNA directly from ATP, GTP, CTP, and UTP, cleaving off two phosphates to power each new bond. The A in DNA has deoxyribose instead and comes from dATP. The same adenosine shows up in NAD⁺, FAD, coenzyme A, and cAMP. One explanation is the RNA world hypothesis: our energy currency and cofactors may be molecular fossils of a time when RNA did everything.

Lactose tolerance is a broken off switch. In most mammals, the lactase gene (LCT) is turned down after weaning, so lactose intolerance in adults is the original state. Tolerance comes from mutations in a regulatory region, located inside the neighboring gene MCM6, that keep the gene switched on. These mutations arose independently several times (in Europe, East Africa, and the Middle East), a textbook case of convergent evolution.

Where I got stuck

Rosalind LIA. At first I didn’t understand what the problem was asking. I mixed up distributions (Bernoulli is one trial, binomial counts successes in n trials, normal is only an approximation for large n), wrote the binomial coefficient as n!/(k!·(n−1)!) instead of n!/(k!·(n−k)!), and confused “exactly k” with “at least k.” What worked: go step by step with my own formula. First compute “exactly k,” then loop i from 0 to k−1 and take 1 − sum. Lesson: solve one case first, then generalize with a loop.
