Week 3: Missing Semester, almost found

Seven lectures in one week and an honest IELTS baseline
Published

October 12, 2026

weekly

I estimated Missing Semester the way every developer estimates a task: “it’ll take a couple of days.”

What I did

  • A deep dive into mitochondria for this blog: why they have their own DNA and how a swallowed bacterium became part of every animal cell.
  • Missing Semester: lectures 2–8. Seven lectures in one week, and the course is nearly finished: one lecture and one bonus topic left. To be honest, I only watched the lectures and read the notes, without doing the exercises. I don’t see much point in inventing artificial terminal tasks for myself — I’d rather practice when a real need comes up.
  • 3Blue1Brown, Essence of Linear Algebra: lessons 7–8 — inverse matrices, column space and null space, and non-square matrices as transformations between dimensions.
  • Khan Academy’s High School Biology every day for 40 minutes. I finished the second unit, on organ systems.
  • Mock IELTS, all four parts, to get an honest baseline for my English plan. I’m around 6.0 overall on a mock test; the goal is 7.5 by next September.
  • Outside bioengineering: three runs this week getting ready for my 5K race on October 18, a lecture on Van Gogh, university biology lectures as a soundtrack for my walks, and a day with my partner’s grandmother for her birthday.

What clicked

  • Evolution is not a design. While studying biology, everything looks perfectly tuned: every system connected to every other, almost like magic. Then it hits you — it’s survivorship bias. We only see the organisms whose variations happened to work; the rest simply didn’t make it.
  • Mitochondria happened once. Every organism that has mitochondria — me, a mushroom, an oak tree, an amoeba — descends from one and the same ancestral cell. A blue whale and the yeast in my bread are distant cousins.
  • “Beyond the Code” turned out to be my favorite lecture of Missing Semester. I expected another lecture about tools, and got an honest talk about the soft skills around programming instead. It’s the lecture I’d recommend to every beginner developer — and after eight years in frontend, I’d recommend it to experienced ones too.

Where I got stuck

The mock IELTS confirmed something obvious: when you don’t live in an English-speaking environment, active skills suffer the most. I read and listen to English a lot, so Listening and Reading held up reasonably well. But I rarely have to speak or write it, and it shows: in Speaking I run out of words when I have to talk for two minutes straight, and in Writing my ideas are fine, but the language stays simpler than I’d like.

What I’m changing

Most of the changes this week are about English: that’s where the mock IELTS showed the biggest gap.

  • Speaking every day, even if only for two minutes. I take one IELTS Part 2 card, set a timer, and talk with my phone recording. Once a week I transcribe one recording and go through the mistakes. Two minutes sounds like nothing, but it’s two minutes more than I used to speak English.
  • Writing on a schedule. One IELTS task every two weeks, alternating Task 1 and Task 2, each reviewed for mistakes.
  • Grammar and vocabulary from the ground up. I’m going through Cambridge’s “in Use” series cover to cover, starting with English Grammar in Use and Academic Vocabulary in Use. They are not exciting, but active skills need something to build on.
  • A real teacher from November. Recordings and books are a good start, but nothing replaces a live conversation.
